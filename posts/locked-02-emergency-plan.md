# Emergency fund? Seriously?! You're smarter than that.

The six-months-in-savings rule is training wheels. It gets one thing right: you need money when life demands it. It gets one thing wrong: it treats every dollar as if it needs to be ready this second. That can leave a large pile earning little or nothing for years.

Build an Emergency Plan by time.

NOW: Keep this month's bills, pending card payments and a true same-day cushion in checking. That money has a job. Don't chase yield with dollars you need to spend today.

TOMORROW: Put the next layer in a liquid holding such as SGOV or a Treasury money-market fund. Sell an ETF during market hours and the cash normally settles the next business day. A money-market fund's redemption cutoff and availability depend on the fund and broker; check them. This is next-business-day money, not money for a bill already due.

THIS WEEK AND BEYOND: Match dollars with a known date to dated holdings. A CD can work if its maturity or early-withdrawal terms fit. A fully funded long box has a fixed payoff at expiration, but an early exit is at the market price, not a guaranteed cash amount. Neither belongs in the tomorrow layer just because you can technically sell it.

Here's the cost of confusing NOW with all six months. Suppose $30,000 sits in checking at 0%. At SGOV's 3.67% 30-day SEC yield (Sep 24, 2026), that's about $1,100 in annual pretax income foregone if the yield held. At an illustrative 7% long-run stock-return assumption, it's $2,100 in expected annual growth foregone. That's a $1,100-$2,100 annual opportunity-cost range for idling the entire $30,000 - not a guaranteed return or the literal cost of waiting one day. The SGOV yield changes; 7% is a long-run assumption, not a one-year forecast. Neither figure includes tax or trading costs.

What about a liquid ETF as the emergency piggy bank? On the settlement clock, SGOV and an S&P 500 ETF work alike: sell on a trading day, normally settle T+1. The difference that matters is what the piggy bank contains when you break it. Short Treasuries can move and aren't insured deposits, but stocks can fall hard right when you need the money. Liquidity tells you when you can sell; it doesn't promise the sale covers the bill.

You can design for that risk instead of pretending it doesn't exist. If the target is $30,000, a stock-ETF layer 25% larger starts at $37,500; after a 20% decline it's worth $30,000. To withstand a 25% drop, start about 33% larger: $40,000 x 0.75 = $30,000. That's a sizing buffer, not crash insurance - a deeper fall breaks it, and $37,500 invested means $7,500 more at market risk. Keep the NOW layer separate either way.

The point isn't to abolish cash. It's to stop demanding instant access from dollars that can wait until the next business day, or a known date. Size each layer for its actual job, then compare the vehicles on after-tax return, risk and availability - the HYSA alternatives piece does that comparison. T+1 means next business day, so a Friday sale settles Monday.

## Assumptions

An emergency plan is a cash-flow design. The timing and loss assumptions matter as much as the yield.

- **Six months and $30,000:** Six months is the conventional rule being challenged, not a prescribed amount. The example holds $30,000 constant at 0% for a year. $30,000 x 3.67% = $1,101, rounded to $1,100; $30,000 x 7% = $2,100. The $1,100-$2,100 range compares two different risk/return inputs, not guaranteed income and not the cost of waiting one day. It excludes tax, spreads and fees. The 3.67% SGOV SEC yield is the article's September 24, 2026 snapshot; it is not today's executable yield. The 7% is a long-run assumption, not a one-year stock forecast.
- **Buffers:** $30,000 x 1.25 = $37,500; $37,500 x 0.80 = $30,000. That withstands a 20% drop. To withstand 25%, divide $30,000 by 0.75 = $40,000, a 33.33% increase. The extra $7,500 in the first version is also at risk. These calculations assume no taxes, transaction costs or other withdrawals before use. A larger loss breaks the buffer.
- **Availability:** T+1 means the next business day after an eligible securities sale, not 24 hours. A Friday sale normally settles Monday only if Monday is a settlement business day. Bank transfer timing, broker restrictions and fund redemption cutoffs can add delay. [The SEC's T+1 guide](https://www.sec.gov/resources-for-investors/investor-alerts-bulletins/new-t1-settlement-cycle-what-investors-need-know-investor-bulletin) describes the settlement rule; it does not guarantee cash in your checking account by a bill's deadline.
- **Vehicle choice:** The NOW layer stays separate. Dated holdings assume maturity matches the spending date; early exits can lose value. Stock liquidity is not protection against a stock-price decline.
