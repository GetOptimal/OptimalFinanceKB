# ThePointsGuy and the rest of the points industrial complex are tricking you.

I fell for it too. I'd see a luxury hotel award next to a $2,800 nightly rate, divide the rate by the points, and declare victory. The arithmetic checked out. What I never asked was whether I would have paid that rate with my own money.

The points world has a convincing calculator: match the hotel, room and dates, subtract the award fees, and report a tidy number in cents per point. That number tells you how an award compares with a listed cash rate. It doesn't necessarily tell you how much you saved. Price only equals value to you if you'd actually pay it.

Points are poker chips. A $500 chip doesn't feel quite like five $100 bills leaving your hand. Neither does a balance of 70,000 points. The abstraction makes it easier to choose things you might never buy with cash, then call the sticker price "savings."

Suppose a resort room lists at $2,800 a night, or 70,000 points, with no resort fee on the award. The headline math says 4 cents a point. Now the honest question: what would I actually pay for that room? My ceiling is $700 a night - above that, I'd book a perfectly good hotel somewhere else. So the points saved me $700, not $2,800. Divide $700 by 70,000: 1 cent a point. A quarter of the headline. Add parking, resort fees or any cash the award doesn't cover, and it falls further.

There's evidence that a sky-high premium price can be disconnected from what travelers actually buy. At Delta's 2024 investor day, its president said that about fifteen years earlier its domestic first-class cabin was 92% full, but just 12% of available seats were sold as paid first-class seats. At the time, Delta charged about 13 times the coach fare for first class. It narrowed the price gap, and by 2024 roughly 75% of those seats were paid. That's a case study in a price changing when a company wanted more people to buy the product. It isn't evidence that nobody pays full price for premium rooms today, and domestic first class isn't a luxury resort.

Nor does it matter who else pays the rate. A company expensing the room, a stranger splurging with their own money - their willingness to pay isn't yours. Your savings are measured against the cash choice you would have made.

TPG does acknowledge personal value. But its headline cents-per-point comparison can still anchor to a price most people would never pay. That's a price comparison, not necessarily your savings.

My test now is simple. What would I book if points didn't exist? What's the most I'd genuinely pay for this room? What cash charges does the award still carry? Take the most I'd honestly pay, subtract the award charges, and divide by the points spent. If the answer disappoints me, I can still take the nice room. I just won't pretend I found $2,800 under the mattress.

The reward isn't proving I got 8 cents per point. It's taking the trip I want without fooling myself about what it cost.

That's why I got out of the points game. My card spend earns cashback now - money that needs no valuation, no transfer partners, no defending. (I wrote about the switch here: [Cash back is king](/blog/cashback-is-king/))
