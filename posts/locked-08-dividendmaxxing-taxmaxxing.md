# DividendMaxxing = TaxMaxxing.

Pay the IRS less. Live more.

When a company pays me a dividend, it sells a slice of my position on its schedule and hands me taxable income whether I wanted it or not. Every dollar of it is taxed. The points world has sticker prices; the dividend world has yield - the same trick: it feels like income, but it's your own capital coming back with a tax bill attached.

Selling shares myself is the same transaction with the controls in my hands. I pick the year, so income lands in a low-tax year instead of a high one. I pick the amount, so it matches my cash flow needs instead of the board's declaration. I pick which lots, so I harvest losses and sell the least-taxed shares first. And best of all, a sale is only taxed on the gain.

Run that math. Sell $10,000 of a stock that's doubled and only $5,000 is gain. In California's top bracket the combined capital gains rate is about 37%, so the tax on my $10,000 of cash is about $1,850 - an effective 18.5%. A $10,000 qualified dividend: $3,700, on every dollar. And if the yield came from a REIT, a BDC or a bond fund, it's not even qualified - it's ordinary income, up to roughly 50% combined. That's the trap inside the trap: the higher the yield, the more likely it's taxed the worst. Same cash in hand. Wildly different after-tax return.

And the kicker compounds at death. Shares I never sell pass to my kids with the basis stepped up - decades of gains, never taxed. The deferred liability doesn't shrink; it evaporates. Every dividend I took along the way was taxed annually for no benefit. Both approaches can end tax-free for my heirs. Only one of them paid the IRS every single year on the way there.

The dividend investor's last stand is certainty: yield is real cash flow. It isn't. S&P 500 dividends fell 21% in 2009 - one in three payers cut. And the yield concentrates you in exactly the wrong places: financials were 30% of the index's dividend income going into 2008, because that's where the yield was. The basket picks the yield; the yield picks the crisis.

So why do smart people swear by dividends? Ask the community and the pillars come out: don't make me sell into a crash; never sell the productive asset; a paycheck and a rule I'll stick with beats an optimal strategy I'd abandon; watch the income grow, not the quote. Notice what those are. Not one is a tax argument. They're self-control arguments - and their own communities admit it. The honest steelman is that guardrails are worth money to someone who'd otherwise panic-sell.

Fine. Buy the guardrails if you need them. Just know what you're paying for: it's not income, it's discipline - and the IRS taxes that lack of discipline at up to 37%, in the years it chooses, on the full amount. Two places dividends genuinely make sense: inside a 401k or IRA, where the tax argument vanishes, and for the person who truly would panic-sell shares. Everyone else is paying the fee without reading the contract.

My rule: own the whole market, sell slices when I need cash, and let the basis step up at the end. The company doesn't schedule my sales. I do.

Pay the IRS less. Live more.

## Assumptions

The comparison is about control over taxable cash flow, not a promise that dividends never belong in a portfolio.

- **The $10,000 example:** The shares doubled, so $10,000 of proceeds contains $5,000 of basis and $5,000 of long-term gain. At the illustrative 37% combined rate, $5,000 x 37% = $1,850; $1,850 / $10,000 = 18.5%. A $10,000 fully taxable qualified dividend at that same rate costs $3,700. These are equal gross-cash amounts, not equal after-tax spending amounts.
- **Tax rates:** About 37% assumes a top-bracket California investor with long-term gains/qualified dividends. Roughly 50% is an ordinary-income scenario, not a rate on every REIT, BDC or bond distribution. Distribution character, deductions, basis recovery, account type and net investment income tax matter. The math assumes a taxable account; an IRA or 401(k) changes the comparison.
- **Inheritance:** A basis step-up assumes qualifying property at death under the law then in force. It is not a promise about future law, every account or estate tax. The comparison also assumes shares can be held rather than sold for spending.
- **Historical dividend claims:** The article uses a roughly 21% 2009 decline, a one-in-three-payers cut claim and a roughly 30% financial-sector share entering the crisis. These need a consistent index, payer denominator and measurement dates. A [contemporaneous S&P release](https://press.abc-directory.com/press/5487) projected a 21.4% full-year decline and described 78 cuts, but that alone does not establish the one-in-three-payers denominator. It described financials at 20.5% at the start of 2009, down from a 30% peak. Do not treat a peak share as every year's share. Historical cuts demonstrate vulnerability, not a forecast of the next crisis.
