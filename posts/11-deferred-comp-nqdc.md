# Deferred Comp: The Senior-Employee Perk Most Eligible People Don't Use

## The Claim

Most retirement tax planning content focuses on what's available to everyone: 401(k) ($23k cap), backdoor Roth IRA ($7k), HSA ($4.15k), Mega Backdoor Roth (~$36k if your plan supports it). For a top-bracket earner, those buckets combined cap out around $70k of tax-advantaged space per year.

Non-qualified deferred compensation, where offered, can dwarf all of those combined — many plans allow deferral of 50–80% of base salary and 100% of bonus, with no statutory cap. The space is enormous, the math is favorable, and it's almost invisible in mainstream personal-finance writing because the eligible audience is small.

The eligibility cap is not a contribution limit; it's whether your employer offers it. Most who are eligible either don't know the plan exists, dramatically under-utilize it, or default-elect because nobody walked them through the math.

I want to be fair to the conventional caution: the "be careful, employer credit risk!" pieces exist for a reason. NQDC participants at Lehman, Enron, and SVB recovered cents on the dollar. That risk is real. What I dispute is the framing that treats the credit question as a reason to avoid, rather than an input to how much to defer.

## The Working

### The math, for a top-bracket earner

The combined marginal rate on the next dollar of W-2 income for a senior tech employee in California:

| Component | Rate |
|---|---|
| Federal (income above ~$610k, 2024 single) | 37% |
| California state | 13.3% |
| Additional Medicare | 0.9% |
| Net Investment Income Tax (on investment income) | 3.8% |
| **Combined marginal on earned income** | **~50%** |

In retirement, with no salary, the same household typically lives in the 22–32% federal bracket and may have moved to a no-tax state. The bracket arbitrage on each deferred dollar: often 15–25 percentage points.

On $200k deferred for 10 years, that's $30k–$50k of pure tax savings before counting the value of tax-deferred compounding on the corpus.

### The compounding side

Modern account-balance NQDC plans let you choose investments from a menu — often the same or overlapping fund lineup as the 401(k). Returns are real, market-based, and tax-deferred for the entire deferral period.

$200k growing at 7% real for 10 years = ~$393k. That growth was tax-free along the way; only the distribution is taxed.

In a taxable account, the same $200k after 50% tax = $100k starting capital, with annual drag from dividends and rebalancing — terminal value is materially lower.

### The space, compared to other buckets

| Bucket | Annual cap (top-bracket, age 50+) | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|
| 401(k) employee deferral | $30,500 | Pre-tax, ordinary in retirement |
| Mega Backdoor Roth (if available) | ~$33k | After-tax in, tax-free out |
| HSA (if eligible) | $4,150 | Triple tax-free |
| Backdoor Roth IRA | $8,000 | After-tax in, tax-free out |
| **NQDC (if available)** | **Often $200k–$1M+** | **Pre-tax, ordinary in retirement** |

For someone earning $800k base + $400k bonus, deferring 50% of base and 100% of bonus is $800k of additional tax-deferred space — more than 10× everything else combined.

### Why most eligible people under-use it

- The plan is buried in benefits portals, separate from the 401(k) flow, sometimes accessible only during a narrow annual election window
- Election decisions must be made before the comp is earned (§409A constructive-receipt rules) — so you elect in November for compensation earned the following calendar year. The forward-looking nature is unintuitive
- Distribution schedules also lock at election — "I'll figure it out later" doesn't work
- No employer match, no automatic enrollment, no nudges — unlike the 401(k), the friction is all on the employee
- HR rarely walks senior employees through the math. Often the plan administrator is a third party who can't give individualized advice

### The distribution lever — the underrated optimization

Most plans let you elect distributions as a lump sum or over 5/10/15 years. Multi-year distribution = bracket-spreading at retirement = lower effective tax rate on the same total.

A $2M NQDC balance distributed as a lump sum drops you into the top bracket on most of it; the same $2M distributed over 10 years stays in the 24–32% range. This single election decision can be worth six figures across a retirement.

Many participants default to lump-sum because it's the obvious choice and don't realize how expensive that obviousness is.

### The state-arbitrage move (where applicable)

Distributions taken over 10+ years from a qualifying NQDC plan are protected from source-state income tax under federal law (4 U.S.C. § 114) — even if you earned the comp in California and moved to Nevada.

For a senior CA earner planning to retire to a no-tax state, this provision alone is often worth more than the bracket arbitrage. The 10-year-or-longer distribution requirement is the price of admission. Lump sum forfeits this protection.

### Worked example: senior tech employee, late career

- Base $700k, bonus $300k, top federal + CA = ~50% combined marginal
- Defers 50% of base + 100% of bonus annually = $650k/year deferred
- Over 5 years: $3.25M deferred, growing to ~$3.7M
- Retires at 60 to Nevada, elects 15-year distribution
- Combined tax rate on distribution: ~28% federal, 0% state
- Tax savings vs. no-deferral baseline: roughly $1.2M–$1.5M over the household lifetime
- This is on top of every other retirement vehicle they're already maxing

## What I Might Be Missing

This is a genuinely powerful benefit. It is also one with structural features that deserve real consideration before maxing — not as reasons to avoid it, but as inputs to how much to defer:

**You don't legally own the assets.** The investments are real and you choose them, but they sit on the employer's balance sheet, typically in a "rabbi trust." A rabbi trust protects against the employer changing its mind; it does not protect against employer bankruptcy. NQDC participants in Lehman, Enron, and SVB recovered cents on the dollar. For investment-grade large employers over a 5–10 year horizon, the credit risk is small but not zero.

**Concentration risk stacks.** If you also hold meaningful equity comp at the same employer, NQDC adds another layer of single-name exposure to your household balance sheet. Worth sizing against your total employer-linked exposure, not in isolation.

**§409A makes the schedule sticky.** Distribution elections are very difficult to change after the fact (the rules require any change to push the distribution out by 5+ years and be made 12+ months in advance). The "I'll just take it earlier if I need it" instinct doesn't work.

**Distributions are W-2 ordinary income — the bracket arbitrage is the whole game.** If you'll retire in a similar bracket to your working years (rare for top-bracket earners, but possible with large pensions or business income), the arbitrage shrinks.

**Plan-document specifics matter enormously.** Change-in-control acceleration, separation-from-service triggers, hardship distribution rules, and investment menu quality all vary plan to plan. The economics of "NQDC in general" are different from the economics of *your* plan.

## Key Talking Points

- For a top-bracket earner with access, NQDC is often the single largest tax-advantaged opportunity in the household — and the most under-utilized
- The bracket arbitrage is real and large. The compounding is real. Both are amplified by the size of the deferral, which has no statutory cap
- The distribution election is half the strategy. Defaulting to lump sum can give back most of the tax benefit
- The state-tax move (10+ year distribution from a no-tax-on-source-state) is one of the highest-yield planning decisions available to mobile high earners
- The credit and concentration questions are real considerations for sizing, not reasons to ignore the benefit
- If your employer offers it and you're in the top bracket, "I haven't looked at it yet" is leaving the largest single tax lever in the household unpulled
