# Exchange funds like Cache: a long waiting game with the AUM meter running.

The pitch: you have $3 million of one stock with a $1 million basis. Instead of selling, you contribute the shares to a pooled fund. Other concentrated holders do the same, and you walk out with a partnership interest in a diversified basket. No sale, no gain, no tax - a contribution to a partnership isn't a taxable event. Day one, you're diversified. Cache built a brand on this, and the mechanics are real.

Now the catch list.

First you have to get in. Funds raise capacity in windows, and acceptance committees cap how much of one name they'll take. The hot stock everyone is trying to unload is exactly the one they don't want. So you apply, and you can sit there waiting for a slot while your concentrated position keeps doing whatever it's doing. The exit you chose for safety can start with a waitlist.

Then the lockup, which is the law, not a policy. If the fund sells your contributed stock within seven years, the tax code hands the gain back to you personally. So funds lock you in for seven years, and if you bail early you can get handed your own stock back. The liquid alternative to selling is liquid in year eight.

You don't pick the basket. Your interest tracks a pool of whatever other people contributed, plus up to 20% in qualifying assets like real estate that the fund holds to keep its status. You swapped a stock you understood for a grab-bag you didn't choose, with a real-estate kicker.

The fee never sleeps - while you're in. Around 0.85% a year, every year, on the whole position. Over 20 years the drag costs more than the diversification is worth: run it against doing nothing and the exchange fund finishes at $37.0 million against $38.3 million for the person who just held. You paid $1.3 million to lose to inertia.

The exit does improve after year seven. You can redeem in kind: the fund hands you a basket of its underlying stocks, the distribution is tax-free, and the AUM fee stops - you're just a person holding stocks now. But your old basis travels into that basket. The exchange didn't eliminate the $2 million embedded gain; it deferred it. The only escapes are selling later or the step-up at death. An exchange fund isn't a tax dodge. It's a tax waiting room with a fee at the door.

What it sells is real: day-one diversification once you're in, zero ongoing effort, no leverage, no tax lots to manage. If the goal is sleeping better this week and you don't mind the handcuffs, it does the job. Just price the job: a waitlist to enter, seven years of lockup, a portfolio you didn't pick, and a fee that compounds past the tax you were avoiding.
