# The most irrational luxury purchase sold as the American dream: a house in a premium California suburb.

Buying the house I rent would cost me $20,350 a month. My rent is $7,200. Same house, same address, same school district. Owning costs nearly three times renting, before a dollar of principal. That's not a close call. It's an arbitrage.

The math, honestly accounted. Buying the house would cost about $2.8 million. With 20% down and an interest-only loan at 6.75%, the monthly unrecoverable cost of owning is roughly $12,600 of interest, $2,800 of property tax, $350 of insurance, $2,300 of upkeep, and $2,300 of opportunity cost on the $560,000 down payment. Call it $20,350 a month, against $7,200 of rent.

Run those numbers through the [NYT rent-vs-buy calculator](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2024/upshot/buy-rent-calculator.html) and it says rent, by a mile. And here's the thing: the calculator works beautifully. Its logic is honest - add up the unrecoverable costs of owning and compare them to rent. In most of America the answer is sane: price-to-rent of 15 or 20, and buying wins if you stay long enough. The tool isn't broken. My market is just that imbalanced. The calculator assumes rent and purchase price come from the same cost structure, and in California they don't: your rent is priced off your landlord's 1988 tax basis, while the purchase price is 2026 retail with a 2026 tax bill attached.

Here's why: Prop 13. My landlord's property tax is assessed on a basis set decades ago - maybe $13,000 a year. A buyer today pays 1.2% of today's price: $33,600. That $20,000-a-year gap is baked into my rent. In premium California suburbs where the typical home hasn't sold in ten years, the entire rental stock is priced off ancient tax bases while the entire for-sale stock is priced at retail. And the asymmetry feeds itself: owners can't move without resetting their own basis, so they stay, so turnover drops, so every rental that exists carries the old economics.

Which means the real arbitrage isn't renting versus buying. It's inheriting someone's 1988 tax basis versus resetting it to 2026. A 100-year lease would capture it perfectly - ownership economics without the reset. Nobody offers one, but a long tenancy in a Prop 13 state is exactly that, informally, renewed every year the landlord doesn't sell.

To be fair, the house doesn't sit still. Give the buyer 3.5% annual appreciation - generous but sane for a premium suburb - and run both balance sheets in cash. The owner pays $18,050 a month in cash carrying costs. Five years in, the house is worth $3.33 million; against the $2.24 million loan, that's $1.09 million of equity. The renter kept the $560,000 and invested it, plus the $10,850 monthly cash difference, at 7%: $1.56 million. Ten years: $1.71 million of equity against $2.98 million. The renter is ahead by $475,000 at five years and $1.27 million at ten - the carrying costs compound faster than the house.

Appreciation narrows the gap; it doesn't close it. Breakeven takes about 6.3% annual appreciation, sustained for a decade, on a $2.8 million house. That's not an argument for buying. That's a bet that the hottest housing market in the country stays the hottest.

And renting has real costs: rent rises while mortgages don't, there's no forced savings, and a landlord sale can end the arrangement. Where the ratio isn't broken, buying wins on schedule in most of the country. This arbitrage exists in specific zip codes, not everywhere.

So the full play in those zip codes: rent the $2.8 million house for $7,200, and invest the difference. That's $10,850 a month in cash savings, plus the $560,000 that never became a down payment, and that compounds. If I rent this house for 5 years with market returns - 7% assumed - that's $1.56 million. For 10 years, that's $2.98 million: more than the house, paid to yourself instead of to interest and taxes. If you want to own housing, own it where rent covers the asset - not where you're buying someone else's tax reset. Let the landlord keep his 1988 basis. He's earning less than he thinks. You're paying less than you should.

And if you buy anyway? It's a luxury to pay for the right to personalize your home. Just like a monogrammed Birkin, it's not a sound financial decision. But it's a luxury you're welcome to treat yourself to.
