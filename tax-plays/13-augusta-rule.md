# The Augusta Rule: Real, Narrow, and Easy to Get Wrong

## Thesis

§280A(g) — the "Augusta Rule" — is genuine: rent your personal residence to your business for up to 14 days a year, deduct the rent at the business level, and exclude the income at the personal level. The problem is not whether it works; it's that the implementations that get audited share a small set of failure modes, and most popular write-ups don't mention them.

**Reader:** Single-member LLC owners, S-corp shareholders, sole proprietors. Anyone who has seen a "deduct $1,500/day for board meetings at your house!" thread on tax-savings X.

---

## Beat 1 — The Claim

- Rent your home to your business for ≤14 days/year
- Charge a fair-market rate for the space
- Business deducts the rent as an ordinary business expense
- Personal income is excluded under §280A(g) — fully tax-free
- Common pitch: **$1,500/day × 14 days = $21,000** of deductions for the cost of issuing yourself a 1099 you don't pay tax on

---

## Beat 2 — The Working

The statute is real and the mechanism works as described — at the floor. The audit failures cluster around a few specific issues:

### Failure Mode 1: No Documentation of Business Purpose

"Annual board meeting" requires a board, an agenda, minutes, and attendees that are actually meeting. A single-member LLC with no employees holding 14 "board meetings" a year invites scrutiny.

### Failure Mode 2: No FMV Substantiation

"$1,500/day" needs comparable rentals — Peerspace listings, hotel meeting room quotes for similar capacity, dated and saved at the time of the rental. The rate must be defensible for your specific market and space.

### Failure Mode 3: Frequency Clustering

14 separate one-day "meetings" looks different to an examiner than 2 weekend retreats. The pattern of usage matters.

### Failure Mode 4: No Actual Rental

A check from the business to the owner with no usage agreement, no rental contract, no exclusive-use record is not a rental — it's a transfer.

### Failure Mode 5: S-Corp Owner-Employee Complications

The rental needs to be to the entity, not a roundabout disguised wage. The IRS can recharacterize payments that lack economic substance.

### Additional Technical Points

- The deduction sits in §162 (ordinary and necessary business expense) — meaning the IRS can challenge whether the meetings were **ordinary and necessary** independent of whether §280A(g) applies on the personal side
- The personal exclusion is hard-edged: **15 days = the entire rental income becomes taxable**, not just day 15
- State treatment varies — some states don't conform to §280A(g) on the personal side, partially undoing the strategy

### The Honest Yield

For a legitimate business with genuine off-site needs: **$5k–$25k/year** of clean deductions. The "$50k+/year board meeting" implementations are the ones that get pulled.

This is one of the cleanest small-business tax moves if run properly. It is also the strategy with the highest gap between what's possible and what people actually substantiate.

## Beat 3 — Where I Might Be Missing Something

- **Recent audit guidance** — IRS has signaled increased scrutiny but I haven't seen large-scale published outcomes
- **Partnership treatment** differs from S-corp treatment in ways I've seen treated inconsistently
- **The "reasonable rate" threshold** has very little case law — most positions rely on contemporaneous comparables, not precedent
- I'd update on: actual audit outcomes from CPAs who have defended Augusta deductions in the last 3 years

---

## Key Talking Points

1. **The strategy is real. The executions that get pulled are real too.**
2. **Documentation is not paperwork — it is the strategy.** Without contemporaneous FMV and meeting records, you have a deduction with no defense.
3. **$1,500/day is plausible for some markets and laughable for others.** The number is not the thing; the comparable is.
4. **The 14-day cliff is unforgiving.** Day 15 retroactively disqualifies the whole year's exclusion.
5. **This is a small recurring optimization, not a structural one.** Worth doing well or not doing.

---

## Scarce Angle

The Augusta Rule has plenty of "here's what it is" coverage and plenty of "$50k tax-free!" coverage. The middle ground — here's exactly how to substantiate it so it survives examination — is mostly absent outside of paywalled CPA material.
