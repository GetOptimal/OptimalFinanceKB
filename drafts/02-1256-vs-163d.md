# The §1256 Tax Treatment That Makes Box Spreads Structurally Cheaper Than Margin

## The Claim

Margin interest is tax-deductible. That's one of the reasons borrowing against your portfolio is efficient — the IRS lets you write off the interest cost against your investment income. Box spreads don't come with a clean interest statement, so they're not obviously better on a tax basis.

This framing is repeated in nearly every personal finance comparison of borrowing strategies. It's not wrong as a headline. But as working analysis for a W-2 earner in a high-tax state, it falls apart almost immediately.

## The Working

### The §163(d) Trap for W-2 Earners

Section 163(d) of the Internal Revenue Code governs the deductibility of investment interest expense — the interest you pay on margin loans or securities-backed lines of credit. The rule says: you can deduct investment interest expense up to the amount of your **net investment income** for the year.

Net investment income (NII) is defined as investment income minus investment expenses. For a typical W-2 earner:
- **Investment income**: dividends, interest, short-term capital gains (not long-term, unless you elect to include them)
- **Investment expenses**: investment interest, advisory fees (largely eliminated post-TCJA), and other qualifying items

Here's the trap: if your primary income is wages and your portfolio holds broad index ETFs with low dividend yields, your NII may be very small — or zero. Long-term capital gains don't count as NII for §163(d) purposes unless you make an election to include them, which permanently converts those gains from the preferential LTCG rate to ordinary income. That's rarely worth it.

**The practical result**: for many W-2 earners, §163(d) deductibility of margin interest is $0. The deduction exists on paper; it's inaccessible in practice.

Let me run specific numbers. Assumptions:
- Filing status: Married Filing Jointly
- W-2 income: $350,000
- Federal marginal rate: 32%
- State: California, marginal rate: 9.3%
- Combined marginal rate: ~41.3% (ignoring phase-outs and surcharges for simplicity)
- Investment portfolio: $2M in broad index ETFs, ~1.2% dividend yield
- Annual dividends: ~$24,000
- No significant short-term capital gains
- Margin loan: $200,000 at 6.5% APR → annual interest: $13,000

Under §163(d):
- NII: $24,000 in dividends
- Investment interest expense: $13,000
- Deductible: $13,000 (NII exceeds interest, so full deduction is allowed in this example)
- Federal tax savings: $13,000 × 32% = **$4,160**
- State tax savings (CA): $13,000 × 9.3% = **$1,209**
- Total tax savings: **$5,369**
- After-tax cost of $13,000 margin interest: **$7,631**
- Effective after-tax rate: **~3.82%**

Now let me push on the dividends. If the investor holds low-dividend ETFs (say, QQQ at ~0.6% or VTI at ~1.3% weighted), or holds primarily in tax-advantaged accounts (where dividends don't show up in taxable NII), the NII might be $5,000–$10,000. In that case:

- NII: $8,000
- Interest deductible: $8,000 (limited)
- Carryforward: $5,000 (deferred, not eliminated, but deferred indefinitely)
- Tax savings this year: $8,000 × 41.3% = **$3,304**
- After-tax cost: $13,000 - $3,304 = **$9,696**
- Effective rate: **~4.85%** — barely below the nominal 6.5%, and approaching it asymptotically in years with low NII

The §163(d) deduction is real but unreliable for W-2 earners with tax-efficient equity portfolios.

### How §1256 Works for Box Spreads

SPX index options are §1256 contracts. The tax treatment is:
1. **60/40 rule**: 60% of gains/losses are treated as long-term capital gains/losses, 40% as short-term, regardless of how long you held the position
2. **Mark-to-market at year-end**: open positions are deemed closed and reopened at fair market value on December 31
3. **Three-year carryback**: §1256 losses can be carried back three years (to offset prior §1256 gains), or carried forward indefinitely
4. **No investment interest limitation**: the loss on the box spread is a capital loss, not interest expense — it flows through the §1256 schedule and is not subject to §163(d) at all

When you execute a box spread loan:
- You receive cash upfront (the premium from selling the box)
- At expiration, you owe the full strike width (the "face value" of your zero-coupon note)
- The difference between premium received and amount paid at expiration is your interest cost, economically — but it's characterized as a §1256 capital loss, not interest expense

For our $200,000 loan example, let's say you execute a 14-month box spread at an implied APR of 5.0%:

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Loan amount | $200,000 |
| Implied APR | 5.00% |
| Term | 14 months (427 days) |
| Gross interest cost (economic) | $200,000 × 5.0% × (427/365) = **$11,699** |
| §1256 characterization | 60% long-term / 40% short-term capital loss |
| LTCG portion of loss | $7,019 |
| STCG portion of loss | $4,680 |

Now let's compare the tax treatment assuming this investor has:
- Sufficient long-term capital gains to absorb the LTCG loss (common for someone doing TLH alongside this)
- Ordinary income to offset with STCG loss (offset at 32% + 9.3%)

Tax value of the §1256 loss:
- LTCG loss offset (federal 20% + CA 13.3%): $7,019 × 33.3% = **$2,337**
- STCG loss offset (federal 32% + CA 9.3%): $4,680 × 41.3% = **$1,933**
- Total tax value of the loss: **$4,270**

After-tax cost: $11,699 - $4,270 = **$7,429**
Effective after-tax APR: $7,429 / $200,000 × (365/427) = **~3.17%**

Compare that to the margin scenario where the deduction was fully accessible (the optimistic case): effective after-tax APR of **~3.82%**.

**The box spread is ~65 basis points cheaper after tax, even in the optimistic margin scenario.** In the more realistic scenario where §163(d) limits the deduction to $8,000 of NII, the margin effective rate is ~4.85% and the box spread is still ~3.17%. The gap widens to ~170 bps.

### The Carryback Option

One feature of §1256 losses that I don't see discussed enough: the three-year carryback. If you had substantial §1256 gains in prior years (from futures trading, other index option positions, or prior box spreads that settled), you can carry back the box spread loss to offset those gains and receive a refund for taxes already paid. This is unusually powerful — most capital loss carryforwards are just deferred; the §1256 carryback lets you actually recover past taxes.

For most readers, the carryback won't apply because you don't have prior §1256 gains. But if you do, model the refund: the effective borrowing rate can drop to effectively zero for the portion of the loss that generates a refund from a prior high-bracket year.

### Assumptions I'm Stating Explicitly

1. **CA state income tax at 9.3%**: CA taxes §1256 losses as regular capital losses (no special 60/40 treatment at the state level — verify for your state)
2. **No NIIT interaction**: At this income level ($350k MFJ), net investment income tax (3.8% on NII) applies. I've omitted it from the base case for simplicity; including it slightly reduces the value of the §163(d) deduction and thus slightly widens the box spread's advantage
3. **Sufficient capital gains to absorb losses**: If you have no capital gains against which to offset the §1256 loss this year, the loss carries forward. This defers (not eliminates) the tax benefit. Model your specific situation.
4. **Bracket stability**: The comparison assumes the same federal bracket in both scenarios. If you expect to be in a lower bracket in future years, the carryforward value is slightly reduced.

## What I Might Be Missing

1. **State treatment of §1256**: I've described CA's treatment. Other states may follow federal 60/40 or may not — especially states with no capital gains preference (where the 60/40 doesn't help). If you know the authoritative treatment in NY, TX (no income tax), WA (capital gains tax enacted 2022), or any other state, please add it to the repo.

2. **The NIIT interaction in detail**: I excluded NIIT from the worked example for clarity, but at high incomes, the 3.8% NIIT applies to NII and can interact with §163(d) in ways I've simplified. If you've modeled this precisely, I'd like to see the comparison.

3. **PLR or case law on §163(d) for W-2 earners**: I've stated the rule as I read it from IRC §163(d) and related guidance.
