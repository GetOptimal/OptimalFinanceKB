# RMD Cliff at 73? Why the Right Move Might Be to Start Smart Withdrawals at 60

## The Claim

The conventional advice sounds reasonable:

- "Just take your RMDs starting at 73 and pay the tax — it's not avoidable"
- "Roth conversions are a wash because you're paying tax now instead of later"
- "Stay in retirement accounts as long as possible — tax-deferred growth always wins"
- The default plan: spend taxable assets first, then tax-deferred, then Roth

I want to be fair to this framing: tax-deferred growth *is* mathematically powerful, and the intuition that paying tax later is better than paying it now is correct in a vacuum. The problem is that retirement tax math doesn't happen in a vacuum. It happens inside a system where multiple tax triggers stack on each other at specific thresholds — and RMDs push you into all of them simultaneously.

## The Working

### The RMD mechanics

The RMD divisor at 73 is ~26.5; it shrinks every year. By 85, you're forced to distribute ~6.25% of the balance. By 90, ~8.8%.

For a $3M traditional IRA growing at 6%, the year-1 RMD is ~$113k. By age 80, even with distributions, the balance is often higher than at 73 and the RMD is larger in dollars. The balance grows faster than you're required to withdraw — until it doesn't, and then you're distributing enormous sums in your 80s and 90s.

### The stacked tax effects

Nobody includes all of these in the headline marginal rate, but they all land together:

| Tax trigger | How RMDs activate it |
|---|---|
| Federal bracket creep | RMD pushes AGI from 22% → 32% or 32% → 37% |
| IRMAA Medicare surcharges | Cliff-based: one dollar over threshold = full tier increase, multi-thousand-dollar Part B + D premium impact |
| NIIT (3.8%) | RMD income pushes MAGI above $200k/$250k threshold, triggering 3.8% on all investment income |
| Social Security taxation | Up to 85% of SS benefits become taxable as provisional income rises |
| State income tax | Many states tax retirement distributions at full ordinary rates |
| Widow's tax cliff | Same income, single-filer brackets after a spouse passes — the bracket compression is brutal |

For a household with $3M pre-tax and Social Security, the all-in marginal rate on RMD dollars can exceed 40% — sometimes approaching 50% when you include the IRMAA cliff effects (which aren't marginal, they're step functions).

### The gap years

The period between typical retirement (age 60–65) and RMD onset (age 73) are often the lowest-bracket years of the retiree's adult life. No salary. Social Security may not have started. Medicare hasn't kicked in (no IRMAA exposure yet). The standard deduction still applies.

A household that retired at 62 with no earned income, living off taxable savings, may have a federal tax rate of 12–22% during these years — compared to 32%+ once RMDs, SS, and IRMAA all stack at 73.

### Two levers available in the gap years

**1. Voluntary distributions** to "fill up" the lower brackets (12%, 22%) before RMDs force you into 32%+. You're paying tax at rates you'll never see again.

**2. Roth conversions** of the same dollars — same tax cost today, but those dollars never have an RMD and never get taxed again. Every dollar converted is permanently removed from the RMD calculation.

### Sequencing matters enormously

A Roth conversion at 65 in the 22% bracket beats the same dollars distributed at 75 in the 32% bracket plus IRMAA plus SS taxation. The gap isn't 10 percentage points — it's often 15–20 when you include the knock-on effects.

The window closes hard: once Social Security starts and Medicare begins, the IRMAA cliff makes large conversions much more expensive. IRMAA uses a 2-year lookback — a large conversion at 63 doesn't hit IRMAA until 65 (the first year of Medicare eligibility).

### Worked example

**Setup:** $2.5M traditional IRA, retirement at 62, Social Security at 67.

**Plan A (default):** Live off taxable assets, defer everything. RMDs hit at 73.
- Year 1 RMD at 73: ~$104k (on what is now ~$2.75M after growth minus taxable spending)
- By 80: RMDs of $150k+ pushing household into 32% bracket
- IRMAA surcharges: $4,000–$8,000/year in extra Medicare premiums
- 85% of Social Security taxable
- Lifetime tax on the pre-tax pool: very roughly $1.8M–$2.2M

**Plan B:** Aggressive Roth conversions ages 62–72, filling the 22% and 24% brackets each year (~$100k–$180k converted per year).
- Reduces traditional balance to ~$1.2M by 73
- RMDs at 73: ~$45k — stays in the 12–22% bracket
- IRMAA: stays below surcharge thresholds
- Social Security: less than 50% taxable
- Lifetime tax on the pre-tax pool: very roughly $1.1M–$1.4M

**Lifetime tax difference: $500k–$800k.** The numbers are household-specific, but the direction is almost always the same for balances above $1.5M.

### This is the rare move where the IRS is on your side

The tax code actively rewards earlier voluntary recognition: there's no penalty for taking distributions after 59½, the Roth conversion mechanism is explicitly provided for in statute, and the gap-year brackets are genuinely low. You're not exploiting a loophole — you're using the progressive rate structure as designed, in the years when your income is lowest.

## What I Might Be Missing

**Tax law changes.** The TCJA expires end of 2025; brackets revert higher in 2026 absent legislation. If the 22% bracket becomes 25% and the 24% becomes 28%, the conversion math still works but the gap-year window is less generous. Conversely, if brackets stay low, the case strengthens.

**State of residence.** If you live in a no-income-tax state now and plan to stay, the state component disappears from the analysis. If you're in a high-tax state during conversion and moving to a no-tax state before RMDs, the math shifts — conversions done *after* the move are better.

**Charitable giving plans.** Qualified Charitable Distributions (QCDs) from IRAs after 70½ are powerful — up to $105k/year directly to charity, excluded from AGI entirely. For a household giving $50k+/year to charity, QCDs from the traditional IRA can be more efficient than converting and giving from Roth. Sometimes powerful enough to make the conversion case weaker.

**Estate planning interaction.** If your heirs are in higher brackets than you, Roth conversions are even better — they inherit tax-free. If your heirs are in lower brackets (early career, part-time), leaving traditional and letting them pay tax at their rate may be better. The SECURE Act's 10-year distribution rule for inherited IRAs changes this calculation significantly — most non-spouse beneficiaries are now forced into a 10-year window regardless.

**ACA subsidies (pre-65).** For early retirees on ACA plans before Medicare, Roth conversions that increase MAGI can blow up premium tax credit eligibility. The ACA cliff at 400% FPL is brutal — one dollar over can cost $15k+ in lost subsidies. Conversion sizing must account for this.

### What I'd update on

I'd like rigorous head-to-head data on conversion strategies that account for IRMAA cliffs *and* SS taxation *simultaneously*. Most published "Roth conversion calculators" simplify these to the point of being misleading — they model the bracket effect but ignore IRMAA step functions and SS provisional income formulas.

If you've built or used a tool that models all three simultaneously, or have case-study data from actual multi-year conversion ladders, open an issue on [GetOptimal/OptimalFinanceKB](https://github.com/GetOptimal/OptimalFinanceKB/issues) with what you've found.

## Key Talking Points

- The RMD problem is not the RMD. It's the stack — bracket + IRMAA + NIIT + SS taxation — that lands together every January starting at 73
- The gap years (retirement to 73) are the cheapest tax real estate of your life. Most retirees don't know they're standing on it
- "Tax-deferred growth always wins" is true in a vacuum. In a household with IRMAA cliffs and SS taxation, it stops being true around the seven-figure pre-tax balance
- The lever is voluntary distributions and Roth conversions during the gap years. You cannot pull it after 73 — the window closes
- This is one of the few major planning moves where doing nothing is actively the wrong default
